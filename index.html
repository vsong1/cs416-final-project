NASA started tracking global surface temperature change in 1980 with GISTEMP, the GISS Surface Temperature Analysis. Since then, the planet's average surface temperature has climbed roughly 1.2°C, with almost none of that rise happened evenly. What follows is an exploration of the record, starting from the ground up.

A flat line for decades, then a climb

For the record's first seventy years, the global anomaly wobbled within about half a degree of its baseline. Then, beginning around the mid-1970s, the ten-year average trend line stops wobbling and starts climbing, and it hasn't leveled off since.

Thin bars: annual global mean anomaly relative to the 1951-1980 baseline. Bold line: 10-year trailing average.

The globe is not warming evenly

Break the same record into latitude bands and the single global line splits apart. The tropics have warmed the most steadily, tightly following the global average. In contrast, the Arctic has warmed explosively. But the Antarctic, insulated by the Southern Ocean's capacity to absorb heat, has lagged behind the rest of the world.

Each line is a 10-year trailing average for its latitude band.
